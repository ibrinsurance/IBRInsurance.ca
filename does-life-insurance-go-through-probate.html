Does life insurance go through probate in Canada?

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 21, 2026 5 min read
Quick Summary
  • No — as long as a named individual is listed as beneficiary, the payout goes directly to them and bypasses probate entirely.
  • In Ontario, that also means the payout is excluded from the roughly 1.5% Estate Administration Tax calculated on everything else in the estate.
  • The one common mistake: naming "my estate" instead of a person — this pulls the payout back into probate.
  • This applies across Canada, though provincial probate fee rates vary — the beneficiary mechanism works the same way everywhere.

The short answer

In almost all cases, no — life insurance proceeds go directly to the named beneficiary and bypass probate entirely, as long as a person (not "my estate") is named as beneficiary on the policy. This is one of the most underused advantages of life insurance in a Canadian estate plan, and it's also where a surprising number of people accidentally undo the benefit without realizing it.

Why naming a person as beneficiary bypasses probate

Probate is the court process that validates a will and confirms an executor's authority over assets that flow through the estate. Life insurance with a named individual beneficiary is a contract between you and the insurer, not an estate asset — the insurer pays the named person directly, on proof of death, without needing probate's involvement. That means faster access to funds (often days to weeks, instead of the months probate can take), and in most provinces it also means the payout isn't included when calculating probate fees, which are charged as a percentage of the estate's value.

Why this matters in Ontario specifically

Ontario's probate fees (officially the Estate Administration Tax) run about 1.5% on estate value over $50,000. On a $1,000,000 estate, that's roughly $14,250 in probate fees alone. A $500,000 life insurance policy with a named beneficiary skips that calculation entirely — it's simply not part of the estate the fee is calculated against. When life insurance DOES go through probate

There's one common way this protection gets accidentally cancelled out:

  • No beneficiary is named, or "my estate" is named directly — in either case, the proceeds become part of the estate and are distributed according to the will, going through probate like any other estate asset.
  • A named beneficiary dies before the policyholder and no contingent (backup) beneficiary was ever added, which can default the payout back into the estate.
  • A corporation, rather than an individual, is named as beneficiary — a different situation with its own tax treatment, covered in our guide to corporate-owned life insurance.

Does this apply outside Ontario?

Yes — the principle that a named-individual beneficiary bypasses probate applies across Canada, though the specific probate fee rates vary by province (for example, Alberta charges a flat fee rather than a percentage, while British Columbia's rates are closer to Ontario's). Whatever the provincial rate, the mechanism is the same: a properly named beneficiary keeps the payout out of that calculation entirely.

A two-minute check worth doing today

Pull up your existing life insurance policies and confirm two things: that a specific person (or people) is named as beneficiary, not "my estate," and that the designation still reflects who you actually want to receive the money — after a marriage, divorce, new child, or other major life change, beneficiary designations are one of the most commonly forgotten updates.

Frequently Asked

Life insurance & probate, answered.

Does life insurance go through probate in Canada?+
No, as long as a named individual (or individuals) is listed as beneficiary on the policy. The insurer pays that person directly, bypassing the estate and probate process entirely. If no beneficiary is named, or the estate itself is named as beneficiary, the proceeds do go through probate.
How much are probate fees in Ontario?+
Ontario's Estate Administration Tax is roughly 1.5% on estate value above $50,000 (with no tax on the first $50,000). On a $1,000,000 estate, that works out to approximately $14,250.
Can I name more than one beneficiary on a life insurance policy?+
Yes — you can name multiple beneficiaries and specify what percentage each receives. You can also name contingent (backup) beneficiaries in case a primary beneficiary predeceases you.
What happens if I name 'my estate' as the beneficiary?+
The death benefit becomes part of your estate and is distributed according to your will, which means it goes through probate and is included when probate fees are calculated — losing the main advantage of naming an individual directly.
Does naming a minor child as beneficiary cause problems?+
It can — insurers generally won't pay a death benefit directly to a minor, and the funds may need to be held by a court-appointed guardian or trustee until the child reaches the age of majority. A trust or a named adult trustee is usually a cleaner solution.
How often should I review my beneficiary designations?+
After any major life change — marriage, divorce, a new child or grandchild, or the death of a current beneficiary — and as a general habit, every few years even if nothing has obviously changed.
