The FHSA: the best way to save for a first home

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 20, 2026 9 min read
Quick Summary
  • The FHSA combines an RRSP's tax deduction with a TFSA's tax-free withdrawal — a rare best-of-both-worlds account.
  • 2026 limits: $8,000/year, $40,000 lifetime, with one year of carry-forward room (max $16,000 in a single year).
  • Unlike the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan, qualifying FHSA withdrawals for a first home have no repayment requirement — the money is simply gone tax-free.
  • You can use the FHSA and the Home Buyers' Plan together on the same home purchase.

How the FHSA actually works

The First Home Savings Account launched in 2023 and was built specifically to solve one problem: first-time buyers needed an account that gave the up-front tax break of an RRSP without saddling them with a future tax bill or a repayment schedule.

It delivers on both halves. Contributions are tax-deductible in the year you make them, just like an RRSP. Qualifying withdrawals — including every dollar of investment growth — are completely tax-free, just like a TFSA, as long as the money goes toward buying a first home.

To open one, you need to be a Canadian resident, at least the age of majority in your province, and a genuine first-time buyer — meaning neither you nor your spouse/common-law partner has owned and lived in a home you owned in the current year or the preceding four calendar years.

Contribution limits & rules

  • $8,000 annual contribution limit for 2026.
  • $40,000 lifetime contribution limit.
  • Unused room carries forward one year only — so with a full year of unused room, you can contribute up to $16,000 in a single year.
  • The account must be used within 15 years of opening, by the end of the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal — whichever comes first.

If a home purchase doesn't happen, the funds aren't lost: they can be transferred tax-free into an RRSP or RRIF (via a separate transfer provision, not using up regular RRSP room), or withdrawn with the amount taxed as income if not transferred.

FHSA vs. the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan

Before the FHSA existed, the main tool for using tax-sheltered savings toward a first home was the RRSP's Home Buyers' Plan (HBP) — a withdrawal of up to $60,000, tax-free at the time, but repayable to the RRSP over 15 years, with unpaid amounts added back to taxable income each year. The FHSA removes that repayment obligation entirely, which is why most planners now treat it as the primary tool, with the HBP as a secondary one that can be combined with it on the same purchase.

A worked example for a couple

Two FHSAs, one home

A couple, each opening their own FHSA and contributing the $8,000 annual maximum, could each reach their individual $40,000 lifetime cap in five years — a combined $80,000 in contributions alone, before any investment growth, all tax-deductible going in and completely tax-free coming out for their first home. That's the real power of the account for a two-income household: it effectively doubles.

Carrying forward unused contribution room

If you don't contribute the full annual limit in a given year, the unused portion carries forward to future years — up to a cap on how much carry-forward room can accumulate. This makes the FHSA forgiving for people who open an account but can't fully fund it right away; the room doesn't simply disappear, it stacks up for when you're able to contribute more.

What happens if you don't end up buying a home

If your plans change, an FHSA can generally be transferred tax-free into an RRSP (subject to your available RRSP room, or without using room if certain conditions are met) rather than being withdrawn and taxed outright. This removes most of the downside risk of opening one early — even if homeownership plans fall through, the money isn't trapped or penalized, it's simply redirected toward retirement savings instead.

Frequently Asked

FHSA, answered.

Can I have an FHSA and a TFSA and RRSP at the same time?+
Yes — all three can be held simultaneously. They serve different purposes, and most first-time buyers end up using at least two of them together.
What happens to my FHSA if I don't buy a home?+
The funds can be transferred tax-free into an RRSP or RRIF, or withdrawn with the amount taxed as income. The account must close by the 15-year limit, by the end of the year you turn 71, or the year after a qualifying withdrawal — whichever comes first.
Can my spouse and I both have FHSAs for the same home purchase?+
Yes — each person can open their own FHSA, which effectively doubles a household's tax-free down payment savings toward one shared home purchase.
Can I use the FHSA and the Home Buyers' Plan together?+
Yes, the two can be combined toward the same qualifying first home purchase.
Do I need to be a Canadian citizen to open an FHSA?+
No — Canadian residency for tax purposes is what matters, not citizenship. You do need to meet the first-time buyer definition.
Does unused FHSA contribution room carry forward?+
Yes, up to a cap on how much can accumulate — if you don't contribute the full annual limit, the unused portion carries forward to future years rather than disappearing.
What happens to my FHSA if I don't buy a home?+
In most cases it can be transferred tax-free into an RRSP rather than withdrawn and taxed, so the money isn't trapped or penalized — it simply gets redirected toward retirement savings instead.
