Critical illness & disability insurance: the coverage most Canadians skip

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 20, 2026 11 min read
Quick Summary
  • About 40% of Canadians become disabled for 90 days or longer before age 65 — a far more common risk than most people assume.
  • Cancer accounts for roughly 67% of critical illness claims by lives insured, with cardiovascular events making up about 33% (Canadian Institute of Actuaries, 2024 study).
  • Disability insurance typically replaces somewhere in the 60–70% range of gross income, not 100% — worth planning around, not assuming.
  • Life insurance pays on death; critical illness and disability insurance pay while you're still alive — which is exactly when most families actually need the cash flow.

Why this coverage gets skipped

Life insurance gets most of the attention because the need is intuitive — if you die, your family loses your income. But statistically, you're far more likely to face a serious illness or an extended disability during your working years than you are to die during them. Critical illness and disability insurance exist specifically to cover that much more common gap, and they're the policies people put off buying — right up until they wish they hadn't.

How critical illness insurance works

Critical illness (CI) insurance pays a lump sum, tax-free, upon diagnosis of a covered condition — typically cancer, heart attack, and stroke, with many policies covering a longer list of conditions beyond those three. Unlike life insurance, the money arrives while you're alive, to be used however you need: paying down debt, covering a mortgage, funding treatment not covered by provincial health care, or simply replacing lost income while you focus on recovery instead of work.

What the real claims data shows

A 2024 study by the Canadian Institute of Actuaries, covering critical illness policies from 11 insurers representing roughly three-quarters of Canada's sum-insured CI business, gives a clear picture of what actually gets claimed:

ConditionShare of claims (by lives insured)
Cancer67%
Cardiovascular (heart attack, etc.)33%

The average benefit paid across the study was approximately $86,518, with the highest average payouts for benign brain tumours (~$105,500), coronary artery bypass surgery (~$98,800), and stroke (~$96,900). These aren't rare, catastrophic edge cases — the study recorded nearly 13,000 claims in total, which is exactly why this coverage exists.

How disability insurance works

Disability insurance replaces a portion of your income — typically not 100% — if you're unable to work due to illness or injury. The exact replacement percentage varies by policy and income level, but it commonly lands somewhere in the 60–70% of gross income range, since paying out the full 100% can reduce the incentive to return to work and increases the policy's cost significantly.

The risk itself is larger than most people estimate: roughly 40% of Canadians experience a disability lasting 90 days or longer at some point before age 65. For a household depending on one or two incomes, a multi-month gap with no income replacement can do as much financial damage as a death would — just without the life insurance payout to soften it.

How the two work together with life insurance

  • Life insurance protects your family's income if you die.
  • Disability insurance protects your income if you can't work, for weeks, months, or years.
  • Critical illness insurance gives you a lump sum, tax-free, the moment you're diagnosed with a covered condition — cash flow precisely when treatment costs and reduced capacity to work collide.

Most comprehensive protection plans layer all three, sized to the household's actual debts, dependents, and income — the same way we approach sizing a life insurance policy with the DIME method.

Group coverage vs. personal coverage

Many Canadians assume the critical illness and disability coverage in their workplace benefits plan is enough, without ever checking the actual terms. It's worth looking closely, because group coverage usually falls short in ways that aren't obvious until a claim is needed:

  • Group disability coverage ends when you leave the job — it isn't portable, so your protection disappears exactly when you might be between employers, self-employed, or on contract.
  • Group critical illness coverage is often capped low — frequently $10,000–$25,000 — far below what a personal policy (commonly $50,000–$100,000+) provides.
  • Group definitions are set by the employer's plan, not by you — a personal policy lets you choose "own occupation" disability wording, which pays out in more situations than a generic group definition.

Group coverage is a genuinely good starting layer — it's just rarely the whole answer on its own, especially for anyone self-employed or running a small business. See our guide to disability insurance for the self-employed if that's your situation.

Return of premium: is it worth the extra cost?

Some critical illness policies offer a return of premium rider — if you never make a claim by a set date (often 15, 20, or 25 years, or at a set age), some or all of the premiums you've paid are refunded. It sounds like a free upgrade, but it isn't: return-of-premium policies typically cost significantly more each month than coverage without the rider, since the insurer is pricing in the eventual refund.

Whether it's worth it depends on how you think about the trade-off: paying more for the certainty of getting money back either way, versus paying less for coverage alone and investing the difference yourself. There's no universally right answer, but it's a decision worth making deliberately rather than defaulting into the version a quote happens to lead with.

Frequently Asked

Critical illness & disability, answered.

What's the difference between critical illness and disability insurance?+
Critical illness insurance pays a lump sum on diagnosis of a covered condition, regardless of whether you can still work. Disability insurance pays ongoing income replacement specifically because you can't work, for as long as the disability lasts (up to the policy's limits).
What does critical illness insurance typically cover?+
Cancer, heart attack, and stroke make up the large majority of claims (about 67% and 33% respectively by category in a 2024 Canadian study), though most policies cover a broader list of conditions beyond those three — the specific list varies by insurer.
How much of my income does disability insurance replace?+
Typically in the 60–70% range of gross income, not 100% — the exact percentage depends on the policy and your income level.
How likely am I to actually need this coverage?+
More likely than most people assume — roughly 40% of Canadians experience a disability lasting 90 days or longer at some point before age 65.
Do I need both life insurance and critical illness/disability insurance?+
They cover different risks and aren't substitutes for each other — life insurance protects against death, while critical illness and disability insurance protect against the far more statistically likely events of serious illness or being unable to work.
Is my workplace group coverage enough on its own?+
Often not entirely. Group critical illness coverage is commonly capped at $10,000–$25,000, well below typical personal policy amounts, and group disability coverage ends the day you leave the job. Group coverage is a solid starting layer, but most people need a personal policy alongside it for full protection.
Is return of premium critical illness insurance worth it?+
It depends on your priorities. Return-of-premium riders refund some or all premiums if you never claim by a set date, but they cost meaningfully more each month than coverage without the rider. It's a deliberate trade-off between guaranteed value back and lower ongoing cost, not a free upgrade.
