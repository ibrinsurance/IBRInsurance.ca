Why self-employed Canadians are the most underinsured group for disability

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 21, 2026 7 min read
Quick Summary
  • Only 25.3% of self-employed Canadians have disability insurance, compared with 57.1% of employees with workplace coverage (StatCan, 2025).
  • Disability insurance typically replaces 60–70% of gross income if illness or injury stops you from working.
  • "Own occupation" coverage matters more for the self-employed — it pays if you can't do your specific job, not just any job.
  • Insurers typically require two to three years of income documentation, so applying is easier once you have consistent financial records.

The coverage gap, in one statistic

According to Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey, only about 25.3% of self-employed workers aged 15 to 69 had private disability insurance coverage — compared to 57.1% of employees who had coverage through a workplace plan. With roughly 2.7 million self-employed Canadians, that gap represents millions of business owners, contractors, and freelancers with no income protection at all if illness or injury stops them from working.

It's an easy gap to fall into. An employee gets group disability coverage automatically, often without ever actively choosing it. A self-employed person has to seek it out, pay the full premium personally, and make the purchase decision themselves — and when business is busy (or slow), insurance is easy to keep putting off.

Why it matters more, not less, when you're self-employed

Employees who become disabled often still have some safety net: short-term disability, a gradual return-to-work program, or at minimum a job to come back to. A self-employed person typically has none of that. If you can't work, the business often can't generate revenue either — so a disability doesn't just interrupt your income, it can interrupt your business's income at the same time, compounding the financial impact.

What disability insurance actually covers

A personal disability insurance policy replaces a portion of your income — typically 60–70% of gross income — if you're unable to work due to illness or injury, for as long as the disability lasts, up to the policy's benefit period (often to age 65 for a long-term policy). For the self-employed, this is often structured as "own occupation" coverage, meaning benefits pay out if you can't perform the specific duties of your own job, even if you could technically do some other kind of work — an important distinction from "any occupation" definitions that pay out far less often.

How coverage works differently when you're self-employed

  • Income proof requirements are stricter. Insurers typically ask for two to three years of tax returns or financial statements to verify income, since there's no employer paystub to confirm it.
  • "Own occupation" coverage matters more. If your income depends on a specific skill set or license, a policy that only pays if you can't do any job leaves a dangerous gap.
  • Premiums are fully yours to pay — but in many cases, premiums for a personal policy can be structured for more favourable tax treatment than you'd expect, which is worth reviewing with your accountant.
  • Business overhead coverage is a separate, complementary product that covers fixed business expenses (rent, staff wages, loan payments) while you're disabled, distinct from the personal income-replacement policy.

If you have a small team: group coverage is also an option

Business owners with even a few employees can often set up a small group disability plan rather than relying solely on personal policies — some insurers offer group coverage starting with as few as two or three employees, which can be more cost-effective than individual policies for everyone covered, owner included. What to have ready before you apply

Disability insurance for the self-employed typically requires proof of income — usually two to three years of tax returns or financial statements — since benefit amounts are tied to documented income, not just a stated figure. Having clean, consistent financial records ready before applying generally speeds up underwriting and avoids a lowball initial offer based on incomplete information.

Frequently Asked

Disability insurance for the self-employed, answered.

How much does disability insurance cost for self-employed Canadians?+
Cost depends on age, health, occupation, income, and the benefit amount and period chosen. Because there's no employer subsidizing part of the premium, self-employed individuals pay the full cost — but the income-replacement value typically still makes it worthwhile compared to the risk of no coverage at all.
What percentage of my income can disability insurance replace?+
Most personal disability policies replace 60% to 70% of gross income, up to the insurer's maximum benefit for your income level and occupation class.
What is 'own occupation' disability coverage?+
A policy definition where benefits pay out if you can't perform the specific duties of your own job or profession, even if you're physically able to do a different kind of work. This is generally more valuable than an 'any occupation' definition, which only pays if you can't work at all.
Can I get disability insurance if I just started my business?+
It's more difficult with very limited income history, since insurers base coverage amounts on documented income. Many self-employed people apply once they have at least one to two years of consistent tax returns to show.
Is disability insurance tax-deductible for the self-employed?+
Generally, personal disability insurance premiums are not tax-deductible, but benefits received are typically tax-free as a result. Business overhead expense coverage can have different tax treatment — this is worth confirming with your accountant for your specific structure.
What's the difference between disability insurance and critical illness insurance?+
Disability insurance replaces a portion of your income for as long as you're unable to work. Critical illness insurance pays a lump sum on diagnosis of a specific covered condition, regardless of whether you're able to work. They address different risks and are often held together — see our guide to critical illness and disability insurance.
