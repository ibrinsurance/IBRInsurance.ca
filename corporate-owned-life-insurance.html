Corporate-owned life insurance: the tax-free exit strategy most owners haven't set up

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 21, 2026 8 min read
Quick Summary
  • Premiums on a corporate-owned policy are paid with lower-taxed corporate dollars, not personal after-tax income.
  • At death, the portion of the payout above the policy's cost basis flows to shareholders through the Capital Dividend Account — completely tax-free.
  • It's the standard way to fund a buy-sell agreement between shareholders, so a business doesn't have to liquidate assets to buy out a deceased owner's estate.
  • It only makes sense once personal insurance needs are covered and there's a real corporate purpose — this is a strategy to build with your accountant, not from a brochure.

Why business owners use corporate-owned life insurance

When a corporation owns a life insurance policy on one of its shareholders, the premiums are paid with the company's dollars — often taxed at the lower small business rate — rather than with dividends an owner would first have to pay personal tax on to receive. That alone makes corporate ownership worth a look for any incorporated owner who already carries personal coverage or plans to.

But the bigger draw is what happens at death. The policy's death benefit is paid to the corporation tax-free, and most or all of it can then flow out to the shareholders' estate tax-free as well, through a mechanism unique to Canadian tax law: the Capital Dividend Account.

The Capital Dividend Account, in plain terms

The CDA is a notional account every private corporation keeps on paper — it isn't a bank account, just a running tally the CRA tracks. When a corporation receives a life insurance death benefit, the amount that exceeds the policy's adjusted cost basis is credited to the CDA. The corporation can then pay that amount out to shareholders as a capital dividend — completely free of personal income tax.

In practice, this means a corporately-owned policy can move a large sum from the business to an owner's family at death with no corporate tax on the proceeds and no personal tax on the payout. For a comparably-sized personally-owned policy funded with dividend income, the owner would have paid personal tax on the dividends used for premiums along the way — corporate ownership defers that entirely to the (tax-free) payout.

Worked example

A corporation owns a $1,000,000 whole life policy with an adjusted cost basis of $150,000 at the time of the owner's death. The full $1,000,000 is paid to the corporation tax-free. $850,000 (the amount above the ACB) is credited to the CDA and can be paid to the estate as a tax-free capital dividend. The remaining $150,000 can typically be paid out as a taxable dividend, or used to redeem shares.

Funding a buy-sell agreement

For businesses with more than one shareholder, corporate-owned life insurance is also the standard way to fund a buy-sell (shareholder) agreement. If one owner dies, the surviving owners typically need to buy out the deceased owner's shares from their estate — often on short notice and for a significant sum. A policy on each shareholder, owned either by the corporation or structured through a cross-purchase arrangement, provides the cash to complete that buyout immediately, without forcing a fire sale of business assets or a drawn-out payment plan with the estate.

Have a shareholder agreement with no funding behind it? That's one of the most common gaps we find.

Other corporate uses worth knowing

  • Key person insurance: protects the business against the financial impact of losing a critical owner or employee, with the corporation as both owner and beneficiary.
  • Sheltering retained earnings: once registered accounts and reasonable operating reserves are covered, a permanent policy can be a tax-deferred place to grow surplus cash inside the corporation.
  • Collateral for a business loan: some lenders will accept a corporate-owned policy's cash value as collateral, which can improve lending terms.
  • Estate equalization for family businesses: if one child takes over the company and others don't, a corporately-funded policy can equalize what each heir receives.

What to weigh before setting it up

Corporate ownership isn't automatically the right call just because a company exists. It generally makes the most sense once personal insurance needs are already covered and there's a genuine corporate purpose — key person protection, a buy-sell agreement, or retained earnings with nowhere more tax-efficient to go. It also means the corporation, not the individual, controls the policy: beneficiary designations, loan access, and surrender decisions typically run through the company, which matters if ownership or shareholder relationships ever change. This is a strategy to build with your accountant and advisor together, not something to set up from a product brochure alone.

Frequently Asked

Corporate-owned insurance, answered.

Is corporate-owned life insurance tax-free?+
The death benefit is paid to the corporation tax-free, and the portion above the policy's adjusted cost basis can be paid out to shareholders tax-free through the Capital Dividend Account. Premiums themselves aren't tax-deductible in most cases, but they're typically paid with lower-taxed corporate dollars rather than dividend income.
Who should own the policy — me or my corporation?+
It depends on your goals. Corporate ownership is usually more tax-efficient for premiums and offers the CDA advantage at death, but it puts the corporation in control of the policy. Personal ownership keeps full control with you but means premiums are paid with after-tax personal income. Many owners use a mix of both.
What is the Capital Dividend Account?+
A notional CRA-tracked account that records tax-free amounts a private corporation can pass to its shareholders, including the portion of a life insurance death benefit above the policy's adjusted cost basis. Amounts in the CDA can be paid out as capital dividends with no personal income tax.
Does corporate-owned life insurance help fund a shareholder buy-sell agreement?+
Yes — it's the most common way to fund one. A policy on each shareholder provides immediate cash to the surviving owners (or the corporation) to buy out a deceased shareholder's estate, without needing to liquidate business assets or arrange financing on short notice.
Can the corporation deduct the premiums?+
Generally no, except in narrow cases such as when the policy is collateral for a business loan. The tax advantage comes from paying premiums with lower-taxed corporate dollars and the tax-free CDA payout at death, not from a premium deduction.
What happens to the policy if I sell my business?+
That depends on how the sale and the policy are structured. In many cases the policy can be transferred, kept by the corporation, or bought out personally as part of the sale — this is worth planning for before a sale is underway, not after.
