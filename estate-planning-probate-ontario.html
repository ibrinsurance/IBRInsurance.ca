Estate Planning

Estate planning and probate in Ontario, explained simply

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 20, 2026 9 min read
Quick Summary
  • Ontario charges roughly 1.5% estate administration tax above a $50,000 exemption — and it applies to anything that passes through probate.
  • An RRSP/RRIF with no surviving spouse is taxed as income in full, in the year of death — a $600,000 RRSP could trigger roughly $270,000 in tax before anything reaches your kids.
  • Life insurance with a named beneficiary bypasses probate entirely, pays within days (not months), and arrives completely tax-free.
  • Insurance solves the liquidity/tax problem — it doesn't replace having an up-to-date will.

What probate actually costs in Ontario

Probate is the court process that validates a will and formally authorizes an executor to distribute an estate. In Ontario, it's charged as an estate administration tax — roughly 1.5% of the value of the estate above a $50,000 exemption threshold. These figures are illustrative of the general structure; always confirm the exact current rate and threshold with a lawyer or accountant, since the specifics can be updated.

The tax applies to the value of everything that passes through probate — which is not necessarily everything you own. That distinction is where real planning opportunity lives.

The RRSP/RRIF tax trap

This is the blind spot that catches the most families off guard. When an RRSP or RRIF owner dies without a surviving spouse, the entire balance is treated as taxable income in the year of death — payable before anything reaches your heirs.

Worked example

A 65-year-old has a $600,000 RRSP. At death, at a roughly 45% marginal tax rate, the estate could owe approximately $270,000 in income tax before anything passes to the children. A permanent life insurance policy sized to that gap costs a fraction of that in premiums and delivers the funds instantly, tax-free, to a named beneficiary — precisely covering the bill the RRSP itself creates.

Why life insurance sits at the centre of the plan

A policy with a named beneficiary does several things a will alone can't:

  • Bypasses the estate and probate entirely — proceeds go directly to the named person.
  • Arrives within days, not the months probate can take — critical when heirs need cash to cover tax bills or living costs.
  • Pays out completely tax-free.
  • Can equalize inheritances — e.g., one child inherits a business, the other receives an equivalent insurance payout.
  • Doesn't become part of the public probate record.

None of this replaces having an up-to-date will — it still governs everything else you own, names your executor, and (if relevant) names guardians for minor children. Insurance and a will work together, not instead of each other.

For incorporated business owners

Estate challenges are amplified when a corporation is involved. Corporate-owned permanent life insurance can fund a buy-sell agreement, pay out through the Capital Dividend Account (CDA) tax-free to shareholders, and help address capital gains exposure on shares at death. This is a short list, not a full explanation — if this applies to you, it's worth a direct conversation rather than a DIY approach.

Frequently Asked

Estate planning, answered.

What is probate and why does it cost money?+
Probate is the court process that validates a will and authorizes an executor to distribute an estate. In Ontario, it's charged as an estate administration tax based on the estate's value above an exemption threshold.
Does life insurance avoid probate in Ontario?+
Yes, when it has a named beneficiary — proceeds pass directly to that person outside the estate and the probate process entirely.
What happens to my RRSP when I die?+
If there's no surviving spouse, the full balance is generally added to your income in the year of death and taxed accordingly, unless a qualifying beneficiary rule applies. This is exactly why many people use life insurance to offset that tax bill.
Do I still need a will if I have life insurance with named beneficiaries?+
Yes — a will still governs everything else you own, from real estate to personal property, and names your executor and guardians for minor children. Insurance and a will work together, not instead of each other.
Is probate different in other provinces?+
Yes — every province sets its own probate/estate administration fee structure and exemption thresholds. The figures in this article are specific to Ontario.
