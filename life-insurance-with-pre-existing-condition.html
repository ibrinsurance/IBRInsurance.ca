You have a health condition. Here's what's actually still possible.

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 21, 2026 7 min read

The myth that stops people from applying

One of the most common reasons people never apply for life insurance is assuming a health condition — diabetes, a past cancer diagnosis, high blood pressure, anxiety or depression, a heart condition — will get them declined outright. In reality, most conditions don't result in a flat decline; they result in a different type of approval, often at a different price. Knowing the options before you apply changes how the conversation goes.

Three paths to approval

  • Standard or preferred rates: for well-controlled conditions with a strong recent history, some insurers will still offer their best pricing tiers.
  • Rated (table-rated) policies: fully underwritten coverage approved at a higher premium that reflects the added risk, rather than a decline.
  • Simplified or guaranteed issue: smaller amounts of coverage with few or no health questions, trading a lower face value for near-certain approval.

What a "rated" policy actually means

When an insurer approves a fully underwritten application with a rating, it's pricing the extra risk your specific health history represents — not refusing to insure you. Ratings are usually expressed as a multiple of the standard premium (for example, "table 2" or "150% of standard"), and the exact rating depends heavily on the condition, how well it's controlled, and how long it's been stable. A well-managed condition with good recent test results and a clean medication history can sometimes bring a rating down significantly, or even qualify for standard rates with the right insurer.

This is where comparing multiple insurers matters most. Underwriting guidelines for the same condition can vary meaningfully from one Canadian insurer to the next — one company's rating for, say, well-controlled type 2 diabetes can be noticeably different from another's. An advisor who can shop your application across several insurers, rather than applying to just one, is often the difference between a steep rating and a reasonable one.

What actually helps your application

  • Recent lab work and specialist records showing a condition is stable or well-managed — this often matters more to underwriters than the diagnosis itself.
  • A consistent medication history without frequent dosage changes, which signals stability to an underwriter.
  • Applying to the insurer whose guidelines actually fit your condition, rather than the first or most familiar name — this is the single biggest lever in the outcome.
  • Being fully upfront on the application. An omission discovered later, even an unintentional one, can void a claim regardless of how minor it seemed at the time.

If you've already been declined somewhere

A decline from one insurer is not a decline from all of them — underwriting standards genuinely differ, and a condition one company treats conservatively might be well within another's comfort zone. It's also not necessarily permanent: time since diagnosis, improved control of a condition, or a few more years of stability can change what's available later. In the meantime, guaranteed-issue options like final expense insurance remain available regardless of health, for smaller coverage amounts.

Frequently Asked

Pre-existing conditions, answered.

Can I get life insurance if I have diabetes?+
In most cases, yes. Well-controlled type 1 or type 2 diabetes with stable blood sugar levels and no major complications often qualifies for standard or lightly rated coverage. The specific outcome depends on the insurer and how well-managed the condition is.
What is a rated life insurance policy?+
A fully underwritten policy approved at a higher premium than standard rates, reflecting the added risk of a specific health condition. It's a form of approval, not a decline — coverage and terms are otherwise the same as a standard policy.
Will I be declined for life insurance if I've had cancer?+
Not automatically. Many insurers will consider an application after a waiting period following treatment completion (often one to five years depending on the cancer type and stage), sometimes at standard rates if the prognosis is favourable, or at a rated premium.
What's the difference between simplified issue and guaranteed issue life insurance?+
Simplified issue uses a short health questionnaire (no medical exam) and can still decline high-risk applicants. Guaranteed issue has no health questions at all and can't decline anyone within the eligible age range, but typically comes with lower coverage amounts and a graded death benefit for the first two years.
Does mental health history affect life insurance approval?+
It can, depending on the specifics — diagnosis, treatment history, and current stability matter more than the fact that a condition was ever treated. Many people with well-managed anxiety or depression qualify for standard or near-standard rates.
Should I apply to multiple insurers if I have a health condition?+
It's one of the most effective things you can do. Underwriting guidelines for the same condition vary meaningfully between Canadian insurers, so comparing offers — ideally through an advisor who can shop several at once — often results in a materially better rating than applying to a single company.
