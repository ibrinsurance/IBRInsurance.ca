How much life insurance do you actually need?

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 20, 2026 9 min read
TL;DR
  • Most Canadians need term insurance, not permanent — it costs a fraction as much and covers the years your family actually depends on your income.
  • A 10x-income rule is a fast starting point, but the DIME method (Debt, Income, Mortgage, Education) gives a far more accurate number.
  • Canadian households are underinsured by an average of 14.5% — and the gap widens to 44% for Gen Z and 32% for women (MyChoice, 2026).
  • Permanent insurance has real uses — estate planning, lifelong dependents, business succession — just not as the default choice.

Term vs. permanent: what's actually different

Every life insurance policy in Canada falls into one of two buckets. Term life insurance covers you for a fixed period — usually 10, 20, or 30 years — and pays a tax-free death benefit if you pass away during that term. It's built to protect the years your family actually needs your income: while the mortgage is outstanding, while kids are growing up, while debts are still being paid down.

Permanent life insurance (whole life, universal life, or term-to-100) covers you for your entire life as long as premiums are paid, and it builds cash value you can borrow against or draw from. That lifelong guarantee and investment component is why it typically costs 5 to 15 times as much as term for the same death benefit.

For most Canadians in their 20s, 30s, and 40s raising a family or carrying a mortgage, term is the right tool — it maximizes coverage per dollar during the years the risk is highest. Permanent insurance earns its cost in narrower, specific situations, which we'll get to below.

Not sure which one fits your situation? A 10-minute conversation is usually enough to tell.

How much coverage do you need? Three ways to calculate it

There are a few common approaches, ranging from rough to precise:

  • Income multiple: multiply your annual income by 5–15. Fast, but ignores your actual debts and goals.
  • Human life value: estimate the total future earnings your family would lose. More precise, but harder to calculate on your own.
  • DIME method: adds up Debt, Income replacement, Mortgage, and Education — the most practical balance of speed and accuracy for most households.

The DIME method, step by step

DIME breaks your need into four concrete numbers you can actually estimate tonight:

LetterWhat it coversHow to estimate it
D — DebtEverything that doesn't disappear when you doCredit cards, car loans, student loans, lines of credit
I — IncomeYears of income your family would need replacedAnnual income × number of years (often 10–20)
M — MortgagePaying off the home so your family keeps itRemaining mortgage balance
E — EducationFunding goals you don't want derailedRemaining RESP contributions, tuition, childcare

Add the four figures together, subtract existing savings and coverage you already have, and the result is a defensible, personalized coverage target — not a guess.

Worked example

A 34-year-old with $15,000 in debt, a $420,000 mortgage, $80,000 income (replaced for 15 years = $1,200,000), and $60,000 remaining for two kids' education works out to roughly $1.68 million in total need. That's the number a policy should be sized against — not a flat "10x income" guess of $800,000.

When permanent insurance actually makes sense

Permanent coverage isn't obsolete — it solves specific problems term can't:

  • You have a child or dependent who will need financial support for life.
  • You're estate planning around probate, RRSP/RRIF taxation at death, or an inheritance you want equalized between heirs — see our estate planning guide.
  • You own a corporation and want tax-advantaged cash value accumulation or a funded buy-sell agreement.
  • You've maxed out your TFSA and RRSP and want another tax-sheltered place to grow money long-term.

Outside of these, term almost always delivers more protection per dollar.

What term life actually costs in Canada

Term premiums are driven mostly by age, health, smoking status, and coverage amount — which is exactly why rates are lowest when you're younger and healthier, and why "I'll get it later" is the most expensive version of this decision. A policy locked in at 30 will be meaningfully cheaper, for the same coverage, than the same policy bought at 45.

Because pricing varies so much carrier to carrier for the same health profile, the only reliable way to know your real number is to compare quotes across multiple insurers rather than taking one company's rate at face value.

Common mistakes that leave families underinsured

  • Relying only on workplace coverage. Group life is usually capped at 1–2x salary and disappears the day you leave the job.
  • Insuring the breadwinner only. A stay-at-home parent's unpaid labour (childcare, household management) has real replacement cost.
  • Letting a term policy lapse right before renewal, when premiums jump — without checking whether a new term or conversion is cheaper.
  • Forgetting to update coverage after a new mortgage, a new child, or a significant income change.
Frequently Asked

Life insurance, answered.

Is term or whole life insurance better?+
For most Canadians, term is the better fit: it's far cheaper and covers the years your family actually depends on your income. Whole life makes sense in narrower cases — estate planning, lifelong dependents, or corporate structures — where coverage needs to last your entire life, not just a working career.
How much life insurance should I have at 30, 40, or 50?+
There's no single number — it depends on debt, dependents, and years of income left to replace. A 30-year-old with a new mortgage and young kids typically needs more coverage (often 10–15x income) than a 50-year-old with a paid-off home and grown children. The DIME method gives a personalized figure instead of a flat age-based rule.
Can I convert term insurance to permanent later?+
Many term policies in Canada include a conversion privilege that lets you switch some or all of the coverage to permanent insurance before a certain age or policy anniversary, without new medical underwriting. It's worth checking before you buy, since not every policy offers it on the same terms.
Does life insurance cover all causes of death?+
Standard term and permanent policies cover death from almost any cause once the policy is in force, including illness and accidents. The main exclusions are typically suicide within the first two years of the policy and death resulting from material misrepresentation on the application.
How long does it take to get approved for life insurance?+
Simplified-issue policies with a short health questionnaire can be approved in days. Fully underwritten policies — which usually offer better pricing for healthy applicants — typically take two to six weeks, depending on whether a paramedical exam, blood work, or doctor's records are needed.
Do I need life insurance if I don't have kids?+
Possibly, even without dependents. A policy can cover a co-signed mortgage or loan, final expenses, a business partnership, or a spouse who relies on your income. If no one depends on your income and you have no debt others are liable for, your need is lower — but it rarely drops to zero.
