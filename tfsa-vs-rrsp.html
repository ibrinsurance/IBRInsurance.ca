TFSA or RRSP: which should you fill up first?

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 20, 2026 8 min read
Quick Summary
  • RRSP contributions are tax-deductible now, but withdrawals are taxed later — it rewards people whose tax rate is higher today than in retirement.
  • TFSA contributions aren't deductible, but growth and withdrawals are completely tax-free, forever — and withdrawn room comes back the following year.
  • 2026 limits: TFSA $7,000/year ($109,000 lifetime if eligible since 2009); RRSP $33,810/year or 18% of earned income, whichever is lower, plus carry-forward room.
  • There's no wrong answer between the two — most Canadians benefit from using both over a lifetime; the question is just what order.

How TFSA and RRSP actually differ

Both accounts shelter your investments from tax while they grow — that part is identical. Where they diverge is when the tax is paid.

An RRSP contribution reduces your taxable income in the year you make it (or you can carry the deduction forward to a higher-income year). Growth inside the account is tax-deferred, not tax-free — every dollar withdrawn, including the original contribution, is taxed as regular income in the year you take it out. By the end of the year you turn 71, an RRSP must convert to a RRIF (or annuity), after which minimum annual withdrawals become mandatory. Withdrawals never restore your contribution room.

A TFSA contribution is made with after-tax money — no deduction. But growth and withdrawals are completely tax-free, permanently, with no forced withdrawals at any age. Withdraw $10,000 this year, and that $10,000 of room is added back on January 1 of next year — not immediately, but it does come back.

The simple rule for deciding

The RRSP's entire advantage depends on one comparison: your tax rate today vs. your tax rate when you eventually withdraw (usually retirement). If today's rate is meaningfully higher, the RRSP deduction is worth more than the tax you'll eventually pay — a net win. If the two rates are similar, or your current rate is actually lower, the TFSA's tax-free-forever structure tends to come out ahead, since you're not deferring tax into a future you can't accurately predict anyway.

  • Higher earner today, expect a lower retirement income → lean RRSP.
  • Early career, lower income, or want penalty-free access before retirement → lean TFSA.
  • Not sure, or income fluctuates → split contributions between both and adjust as your income becomes clearer.

A worked example

Someone earning $95,000/year is likely sitting at a meaningfully higher marginal tax rate than they'll have in retirement. A $10,000 RRSP contribution reduces their taxable income by that amount in the year it's made.

Illustrative, not a guarantee

At a combined federal + provincial marginal rate in the ballpark of 35–40% (actual rate depends on province and total income), a $10,000 RRSP contribution could generate a tax refund in the rough range of $3,500–$4,000 — money that can be reinvested, often back into the RRSP or a TFSA. A $10,000 TFSA contribution generates no refund today, but the full $10,000 (plus all growth) comes out completely tax-free whenever it's withdrawn, with no RRIF conversion or minimum-withdrawal rules later in life.

Using either one for a first home

The RRSP's Home Buyers' Plan (HBP) lets a first-time buyer withdraw up to $60,000 tax-free from an RRSP toward a home, repayable to the RRSP over 15 years (unpaid amounts get added back to taxable income). It's a useful lever, but it's not the most efficient tool purpose-built for this goal anymore — that's now the FHSA, which has no repayment requirement at all and can even be combined with the HBP for the same purchase.

Frequently Asked

TFSA vs. RRSP, answered.

Can I have both a TFSA and an RRSP?+
Yes — they're not mutually exclusive. Most Canadians benefit from using both over a lifetime; the real question is just which one to prioritize first with limited contribution dollars in a given year.
What happens if I over-contribute?+
The CRA charges a 1% per month penalty tax on the excess amount in a TFSA. For an RRSP, there's a lifetime $2,000 grace amount before the same 1%/month penalty applies to any excess above that.
Does unused room carry forward?+
Yes, for both. TFSA room is cumulative since 2009 (or since you turned 18, whichever is later). RRSP room carries forward indefinitely if you don't use it in a given year.
Should I use my TFSA or RRSP for a house down payment?+
The FHSA is generally the better purpose-built tool for this now, with the RRSP's Home Buyers' Plan as a combinable option. A TFSA remains a flexible fallback, with no repayment requirement at all on what you withdraw.
Which one should a 25-year-old just starting their career prioritize?+
Generally the TFSA first, while income and marginal tax rate are lower, shifting more toward the RRSP as income (and the tax rate on each extra dollar) rises. This is a general tendency, not a fixed rule for every situation.
