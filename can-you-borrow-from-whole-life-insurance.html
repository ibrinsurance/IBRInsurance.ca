Can you borrow from your whole life insurance policy? Yes — here's what it actually costs.

By Rattan Amol Taggar Updated Oct 21, 2026 7 min read
Quick Summary
  • Most insurers let you borrow up to 100% of your policy's available cash value — no credit check, no medical exam.
  • Interest accrues on the loan and compounds if unpaid — a $50,000 loan at 8% can grow past $65,000 in a few years.
  • Borrowing itself isn't taxable, but tax can arise on surrender or lapse, calculated as cash value minus the policy's Adjusted Cost Basis.
  • An unpaid loan can eventually cause the policy to lapse, ending coverage and triggering a tax bill at the worst possible time.

How a policy loan actually works

Once a whole life policy has built up cash value, most insurers let you borrow against it — typically up to 100% of the available cash surrender value, depending on the insurer. There's no credit check and no medical exam, since the loan is secured by value already inside your own policy. The money can be used for anything: a business opportunity, a down payment, an emergency, or simply supplementing retirement income.

Crucially, a policy loan isn't a withdrawal — your cash value isn't being removed from the policy, it's being used as collateral while the insurer lends you money against it. That distinction matters for how it's taxed, which we'll get to below.

What it costs

Policy loans aren't free money. Interest accrues on the outstanding balance at a rate set in your contract — fixed or variable depending on the insurer — and if it isn't paid, it compounds and gets added to the loan balance. Left unpaid for several years, a loan can grow substantially: a $50,000 loan at 8% interest can grow to $65,000 or more if simply left to accumulate, which eats directly into the death benefit your beneficiaries would otherwise receive.

Some banks also offer a separate type of loan — a collateral loan against your policy, priced closer to typical lending rates — as an alternative to borrowing directly from the insurer. It's worth comparing both options before choosing.

The tax treatment, in plain terms

This is the part people get wrong most often:

  • Borrowing is not taxable while the policy remains in force — taking out a loan itself doesn't trigger income tax.
  • Tax arises on a "disposition" — surrendering the policy or letting it lapse — calculated as the cash value minus the policy's Adjusted Cost Basis (ACB), not the loan amount itself.
  • The loan balance reduces what's left at surrender, lapse, or death, but it's the gain over ACB that's taxed, not the loan.

You can typically request your policy's current Adjusted Cost Basis from your insurer each year — it's worth knowing before you borrow, not after.

The real risk: an unpaid loan causing a lapse

If an outstanding loan plus accumulated interest grows large enough to approach or exceed the policy's cash value, the policy can lapse — ending your coverage and triggering a taxable disposition (the cash value at lapse minus the Adjusted Cost Basis becomes taxable income) at the worst possible moment, often when the person can least afford an unexpected tax bill. Insurers are generally required to send warning notices before this happens, but it's a risk that's entirely avoidable with a plan to manage the loan.

Before you borrow, ask these questions

  • What's the current interest rate, and is it fixed or variable?
  • Do I have a realistic plan to repay it, or am I comfortable with it reducing my death benefit permanently?
  • Have I checked my policy's current Adjusted Cost Basis, to understand the tax exposure if I ever surrender or lapse?
  • Would a bank loan, line of credit, or a collateral loan against the policy (rather than borrowing directly from the insurer) be cheaper?
Frequently Asked

Policy loans, answered.

Can I borrow money from my whole life insurance policy?+
Yes, once it has built sufficient cash value — typically up to 100% of the available cash surrender value, depending on the insurer. No credit check or medical exam is required since the loan is secured by the policy's own cash value.
Is a life insurance policy loan taxable?+
Borrowing itself is not taxable while the policy stays in force. Tax can arise if the policy is later surrendered or lapses — the taxable amount is the cash value at that point minus the policy's Adjusted Cost Basis, not the loan balance itself.
What happens if I don't repay a policy loan?+
Unpaid interest compounds and is added to the loan balance. If the growing loan approaches or exceeds the policy's cash value, the policy can lapse, ending coverage and triggering a taxable disposition based on cash value minus ACB.
Does a policy loan reduce my death benefit?+
Yes — any outstanding loan balance, plus accrued interest, is deducted from the death benefit paid to your beneficiaries if you pass away before repaying it.
What's the difference between a policy loan and a withdrawal?+
A policy loan borrows against the cash value as collateral — the cash value itself stays in the policy, growing, while you owe the insurer the loan amount plus interest. A withdrawal actually removes money from the cash value, which can have different and sometimes more immediate tax consequences depending on the policy.
Can I get a bank loan using my life insurance as collateral instead?+
In some cases, yes — certain lenders will accept a policy's cash value as collateral for a separate loan, sometimes at a lower interest rate than borrowing directly from the insurer. It's worth comparing both before deciding.
